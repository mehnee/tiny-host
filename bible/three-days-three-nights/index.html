The sign of Jonah · Nisan 14, AD 31

Three Days and Three Nights

Christ was crucified on the fourth day of the week and laid in the tomb as that day's light was ending. He lay there three whole days and three whole nights, and rose as the light of the seventh day ended. When the first day of the new week dawned, the tomb was already empty. Laid over the six days of creation, the week falls into place without a single partial day to stretch.

For as Jonas was three days and three nights in the whale's belly; so shall the Son of man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth.Matthew 12:40

Counted in light and dark

Three of each, with nothing partial

Christ counts days and nights as separate things: τρεῖς ἡμέρας καὶ τρεῖς νύκτας (treis hēmeras kai treis nyktas, "three days and three nights"). The strip below counts exactly that — the tomb's nights and days, from the burial as the cross day's light ended to the rising as the Sabbath's light ended. The brackets above it are the numbered days of the creation week, each made of one night and one day. Scripture does not say whether a numbered day opens with its evening or its morning, so both bracketings can be shown.

Evening to evening. Each numbered day opens with its night. He was laid in the tomb as day 4 closed, so day 5 began with Him already there; Night 1 and Day 1 of the tomb make up day 5. He rose at evening twilight as day 7 closed — the third day in the tomb — when the three days and three nights were complete, and day 8 began with the tomb already empty. The tomb count is the same on either bracketing.

Morning to morning. Each numbered day opens with its light, and an evening keeps the name of the day that is ending (Exod 12:18; Lev 23:32). Night 1 of the tomb still carries day 4's name. He rose at evening twilight, at the close of day 7's light — still day 7 by name, the third day in the tomb — and day 8 began at the dawn when the women came. The tomb count is the same on either bracketing.

God named יוֹם (yom, "day") and לַיְלָה (laylah, "night") apart, and Christ counts them apart. Jonah's count is the same: שְׁלֹשָׁה יָמִים וּשְׁלֹשָׁה לֵילוֹת (sheloshah yamim ushloshah leylot, "three days and three nights," Jonah 1:17). The Gospels do not narrate the moment of rising; the count places it.

The big picture

One week, told twice

Seen from above, the creation week holds the whole story. The passion week tells it in days: the Light of the world is put to death on the day the lights were made, lies still through the day God rested, and the new week begins with an empty tomb. The ages tell it on a larger scale: the cross ends the fourth day, the gospel fills the fifth and sixth, a rest remains on the seventh, and all things are made new on the eighth. The shape does not depend on any chronology.

Day 6: the image given, the image completed

On the sixth day man is made in God's image. The age that answers to the sixth day ends with the redeemed brought into that image, and Christ stands first among them: the firstborn from the dead and the firstborn among many brethren. An observation still being tested.

For whom he did foreknow, he also did predestinate to be conformed to the image of his Son, that he might be the firstborn among many brethren.Romans 8:29

At a thousand years a day

On the larger scale the days are read as millennia: days 1–4 from the fall to the cross, days 5–6 the church age, day 7 the Millennium, day 8 the new creation. Where exactly each day ends depends on the chronologies. The shape is firm; the calendar is held loosely.

But, beloved, be not ignorant of this one thing, that one day is with the Lord as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day.2 Peter 3:8
After two days will he revive us: in the third day he will raise us up, and we shall live in his sight.Hosea 6:2

Checks the week passes

  • "After two days is the feast of the passover" (Matt 26:2), spoken on Tuesday after the Olivet discourse, points to the feast on Thursday. Mark names it in full: τὸ πάσχα καὶ τὰ ἄζυμα (to pascha kai ta azyma, "the passover and the unleavened bread," Mark 14:1).
  • The entry has to be on Sunday. Not on Saturday: the crowd "cut down branches off the trees, and strawed them in the way" (Mark 11:8), and the law put to death "a man that gathered sticks upon the sabbath day" (Num 15:32-36); the Pharisees' only objection that day was to the praise, "Master, rebuke thy disciples" (Luke 19:39). Not on Friday: Monday's cleansing of the market (Mark 11:15) would then fall on the Sabbath.
  • In John, "the passover" is the feast (John 18:28), and the day before it is "the preparation of the passover" (19:14). So "six days before the passover" (12:1) counts back from Nisan 15 to Friday, Nisan 9.
  • On the High Day only food could be prepared: "no manner of work shall be done in them, save that which every man must eat" (Exod 12:16). The spices had to wait for Friday.

Two hard points

  • The supper. Mark dates its preparation to "the first day of unleavened bread, when they killed the passover" (Mark 14:12). John places the supper "before the feast of the passover" (13:1), and the next morning the Jews still meant to "eat the passover" (18:28). This week follows John: the supper opened Nisan 14, and Christ died as the lambs were killed. Every chronology, Friday included, has to reconcile the two.
  • The hour. "The third hour" at the crucifixion (Mark 15:25) stands beside "about the sixth hour" at Pilate's judgment (John 19:14). The usual answer is that John counts the hours from midnight. This is not settled.

The anointing at Bethany is placed by John's time marker. Mark and Matthew tell it after "after two days" (Mark 14:3; Matt 26:6), and many read it as the same event told out of order.

The burial and the rising

Both at the end of a day's light

Scripture names an evening after the day whose light is ending. The burial evening is still "the preparation," and the Sabbath had not yet begun, so He was laid in the tomb in the last light of day 4. The Sabbath is kept from evening to evening, so the weekly Sabbath closed as day 7's light ended: 72 hours after the burial.

And now when the even was come, because it was the preparation, that is, the day before the sabbath…Mark 15:42

Two Sabbaths that week

A working day between two days of rest

Mark has the women buy spices after a Sabbath. Luke has them prepare spices and then rest on the Sabbath. With one Sabbath those two statements collide. With the High Sabbath of Nisan 15 and the weekly Sabbath of Nisan 17, Friday sits between them as the working day.

Why Friday cannot hold

Granting every day, Friday still has two nights

Count Friday's last minutes as a whole day, and the nights between Friday afternoon and Sunday dawn are still two. A third comes only by counting the night before the cross, which Christ spent in Gethsemane and under arrest (Matt 26:36–57), not in the heart of the earth. The rabbinic rule that a touched day-and-night counts as whole counts calendar units; Christ counts the light and the dark themselves.

To make "after three days" and "the third day" true at the same time, it must be evening twilight

The Gospels report one saying of Christ both ways. At Caesarea Philippi, Mark writes "after three days" where Matthew and Luke write "the third day," so the two phrases name one moment. "After three days" needs the three days complete; "the third day" needs it still to be the third day. Only the close of the third day satisfies both.

…and be killed, and after three days rise again.Mark 8:31

Mark's phrase, μετὰ τρεῖς ἡμέρας (meta treis hēmeras, "after three days"), is the Septuagint's wording at Joshua 3:2 for the Hebrew מִקְצֵה שְׁלֹשֶׁת יָמִים (miqtseh sheloshet yamim, "at the end of three days"). "After three days" is the end of the three days; "the third day" is a point within it.

Saying, Sir, we remember that that deceiver said, while he was yet alive, After three days I will rise again. Command therefore that the sepulchre be made sure until the third day…Matthew 27:63–64

The Pharisees use the same pair for one deadline. On Wednesday the two meet at evening twilight on the third day without stretching either. On Friday, "after three days" has to shrink to about 36 hours.

Dawn on the first day

The women came at sunrise; He had already risen

Matthew's "dawn" marks when the women arrived, the same visit Mark, Luke and John place at sunrise. The verb ἐπιφώσκω (epiphōskō, "to grow light, to dawn") names a day coming on and takes its hour from the context: Luke uses it for the Sabbath coming on at evening, during the burial (Luke 23:54). No hour of the first day found Christ in the tomb.

The rest of the sign

Jonah's forty days, this generation's forty years

Christ names the three days, then turns in the next breath to Nineveh, which repented inside Jonah's forty-day warning. The sign is given to "this generation," γενεά (genea, "generation"), and Scripture sizes a judged generation at forty years. Counted inclusively from the cross, only AD 31 reaches AD 70 in forty.

Precedent, not the ground

Why the picture is more than tempting

A picture this neat has to be checked where it could have failed. These parts could have, and did not:

  • The passion week. Had the text required a Friday, the cross would not stand on day 4. It fixes the day on its own: three nights and two Sabbaths.
  • The forty years. AD 31 had to reach AD 70 in forty years; the neighbouring years do not.
  • The cross as completion. John's own wording — "In the beginning" at the start of his Gospel, "It is finished" at the end — reaches the cross as the completion of a creation without the week, and meets it there.
  • The seventh day. Hebrews reads it forward as a rest still to come; the picture did not have to.

The part that stays only tempting is the calendar of the ages — exactly when the sixth day ends. The text cannot check it, and dates set on it have come and gone before.

When does the day begin?

Genesis 1:5 counts עֶרֶב (erev, "evening") and בֹּקֶר (boqer, "morning") as the first day without saying which one opens it. Scripture names an evening after the day whose light is ending: "the fourteenth day of the month at even" (Exod 12:18), "the ninth day of the month at even" (Lev 23:32). That favors a morning start. John calls the dark before dawn "the first day of the week" (John 20:1) and the evening after it "the same day" (John 20:19), which fits neither reckoning cleanly.

Either way He rose on the third day, at evening twilight as its light ended, and the first day of the week found the tomb already empty.
